Your Manager's Belief in You Is a Performance Variable

June 2026 · 5 minute read · First published on LinkedIn

In 1969 J. Sterling Livingston wrote that a manager's expectations are the key to a subordinate's performance and development. More than fifty years later, most organisations have still not acted on it.

What Rosenthal and Jacobson found in 1968

In 1968 psychologists Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson ran an experiment in a school. They told teachers which students were about to bloom intellectually. The students had in fact been picked at random. By the end of the year, those students had on average gained more than their classmates. Not because they were smarter. Because someone believed they were.

They called it the Pygmalion effect: a self-fulfilling prophecy, where the belief of the person in authority becomes the reality of the person underneath them.

The finding has since been tested in workplaces, most rigorously in military training units. The effect is real, and in some studies it is large. But it is not uniform. It is strongest in military settings, among men, and for people their leaders initially expected little from. And when researchers tried to train ordinary managers to produce it through workshops, across seven field experiments, the effect was small.

Belief on its own does nothing. It works through what the manager actually does differently.

Belief changes how you show up for someone. The opportunities you offer. The feedback you give. The patience you extend. The ceiling you set, or refuse to set.

The reverse can happen too. When a manager has already decided who you are, you may eventually perform down to that decision. Not because you are incapable, but because the conditions that enable capability were never created.

What this looks like in a real workplace

I have seen both sides of this across multiple organisations.

The manager who believes in their team asks different questions. Not "are you done yet?" but "what do you need to get there?" Not "why did this go wrong?" but "what did you learn, and what do we do differently?" They give stretch assignments before someone feels ready. They advocate in rooms the person never enters. They set goals that are honest about the timeline and generous about the support.

The manager who has already decided watches for confirmation of what they already believe. They interpret a mistake as evidence of limitation. They interpret silence as disengagement. They withhold opportunity because they are not sure the person can handle it. And in doing so they help create the very outcome they feared.

This is rarely deliberate. That is what makes it so dangerous.

What I want managers to take from this

Look past the role someone was hired for. Every person on your team has a range, a floor and a ceiling. Your job is not to manage them at their floor. Your job is to understand where their ceiling actually is, and then build the conditions that get them there.

If someone needs training, set realistic goals with honest timelines. Tell them what good looks like. Tell them when they can expect to get there. Then give them the resources and the support to make it happen. That is not soft management. That is how you build a team that actually performs.

One caution. Belief is not the same as inflated praise. The research points to expectations that are high and credible, backed by real support. Telling someone they are brilliant while changing nothing about how you lead them is not the Pygmalion effect.

What I want employees to take from this

If you have navigated hard things before, this is no different. You have evidence of your own resilience, even if the person above you cannot see it yet. Every difficult thing you have moved through is proof that you can move through difficult things.

And even if you do not get there perfectly, you will have learned something. Skills compound. Experience compounds. The person you become in the attempt is worth more than the outcome of any single role.

The most expensive thing in your organisation right now

It might not be your attrition rate. It might not be your engagement score. It might be the potential sitting untouched in every person whose manager has already decided they are average. That potential does not show up on any dashboard. But it costs you every single day.

The person standing behind you, believing in you, changes what is possible in front of you. Who is standing behind your team right now?

Evidence notes. Rosenthal, R. & Jacobson, L. (1968), Pygmalion in the Classroom. Original study; its findings remain contested. Jussim, L. & Harber, K. D. (2005), Personality and Social Psychology Review, 9(2): across 35 years of classroom research, expectancy effects are real but typically small and tend to fade rather than accumulate. McNatt, D. B. (2000), Journal of Applied Psychology, 85(2): meta-analysis of 17 management studies, average effect d = 1.13, stronger in the military, with men, and where low expectations were initially held. Kierein, N. M. & Gold, M. A. (2000), Journal of Organizational Behavior: meta-analysis of work organisations, d = 0.81. Eden, D. et al. (2000), The Leadership Quarterly, 11(2): seven field experiments training managers in Pygmalion leadership, small mean effect r = .13. Livingston, J. S. (1969), "Pygmalion in Management", Harvard Business Review, cited as history, not evidence. Workplace observations are my own practitioner experience.
